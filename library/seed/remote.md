# Remote travel

**Licence:** CC BY 4.0, Australian Survival LLM field notes. Not a parks permit and not a road report.
**Confirm with:** the land manager for the track you are on, and a printed map. Conditions close roads. A model cannot see them.

## Before you leave the bitumen

Tell someone the route, the vehicle, who is in it, and when you will call them. Give them a time at which they should actually call for help, not "sometime tomorrow". Mobile coverage dies between towns. Do not plan on it.

Carry more water than a mild day suggests. In hot inland country the limit is water, then fuel, then tyres. A common vehicle habit is a jerry can of fuel and a jerry can of water, both strapped, neither in the cabin to fume or to burst. Know which is which.

A personal locator beacon or a satellite messenger is the difference on a remote track. Register the beacon. Know which pouch it is in. UHF radio is for convoys and some stations. It is not a phone to 000. HF and satellite are for when there is no one on the UHF channel.

Take a paper map. A phone map that needed a download you skipped is a blank screen. A spare tyre you have not practised changing is a suggestion.

## If the vehicle stops

On a remote track, stay with the vehicle unless you can see the help and walk to it in the cool, with water, on a road someone will drive. The vehicle is bigger than you are from the air, it has shade if you rig it, and it is where searchers look. Walk only with a plan to come back, in the cool hours, and leave a note on the windscreen: direction, time, when you will return.

Do not cross a flooded causeway because the town is closer on the other side. See the flood note.

Heat is the medical problem. Shade, water, rest. A person who is confused in the heat needs cooling and emergency help, not another kilometre. See the heatwave note.

## Creek crossings and washaways

Walk the crossing first if you can do it safely. Wheel tracks going in are not a depth gauge. Fast water, a soft bottom, or water over the bumper is a no. In the wet season in the north, a creek that was a trickle at lunch can be a river by dark. Camp on the side you can leave from.

## Fires and the bush

Total fire bans and park rules override any desire for a campfire. A fire that escapes you in dry grass is a bushfire with your name on it. Where a fire is legal, clear a wide circle to mineral earth, keep it small, and drown it until the ground is cold. See the bushfire note before you travel on a bad fire day. Sometimes the plan is to not go.
