# Bushfire

**Confirm with:** your state fire service. NSW Rural Fire Service: https://www.rfs.nsw.gov.au/

## Leave early

Leaving early is the safest choice. The dangerous mistake is waiting to see the flame. On a day of Extreme or Catastrophic fire danger, the plan for most households is to be gone before a fire starts, not to watch a map and hope the wind holds. Fire Danger Ratings in Australia are Moderate, High, Extreme and Catastrophic. Do not treat a model, this note, or yesterday's rating as today's rating. Check the fire service.

A warning may not reach you. Phones fail, apps need a signal, and a fire can start on your road. Have a trigger that does not depend on an official text: a rating, a smell of smoke, a power failure on a bad day, or a time you agreed the night before.

## Before the season

Walk the house once, in daylight. Clear leaves from gutters and the roof, move the woodpile and the gas bottles away from walls, and cut long grass against the building. A garden hose is not a fire plan. Petrol pumps and electric pumps both fail if you have left, or if the power is off. Know two ways out by car, and a place you will actually go: a relative in town, not "somewhere".

Pack a box you do not have to think about: woollen or cotton clothes that cover skin, sturdy shoes, water, medicines, phone charger, radio, important papers or photos already copied, and leads for animals. Synthetics melt onto skin. Do not wait to find the passports.

## If a fire is already going

Follow the instruction you have been given: Advice, Watch and Act, or Emergency Warning. The words differ slightly by state. An Emergency Warning means you are in danger and need to act now. If the advice is to leave and you can still see the road, leave. Do not drive through thick smoke. If you cannot see the road edge, you will hit a car, a tree or a drop.

Sheltering in a building is a last resort, not a tactic to recommend to someone who could have left. If you are trapped, a solid building is safer than a car in the open and safer than a water tank you cannot breathe inside. Stay low, out of the radiant heat, on the side away from the fire until the front has passed, then get out of the building if it is burning. This is a bad position. The plan is to not be in it.

Embers, not the flame front, destroy most houses. They get into gutters, under decks and through broken windows, often after the front has gone past. If you are still there after the front, patrol for spot fires only if you can do it without being burned, and only for as long as you said you would.

## Smoke and power

Bushfire smoke is a health problem for babies, older people, and anyone with asthma or heart disease. Stay inside with windows shut if that is the official advice, and follow the person's own asthma plan. A P2 respirator helps some people; it is not a reason to stay in a fire.

Treat every fallen powerline as live. Stay well back, more than a bus length, and do not touch a fence or a car the line is on. Call 000. Rooftop solar can still be live in daylight when the grid is off. Do not climb the roof or cut cables. Tell firefighters the house has solar.

## After

Do not go home until you are told the road is open. Houses, sheds and trees fall later. Water tanks and creeks may be fouled with ash, dead animals and chemicals from burnt sheds. Do not assume tank water is drinkable. See the drinking-water note.
