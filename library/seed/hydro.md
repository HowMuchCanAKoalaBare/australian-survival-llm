# Creek micro-hydro and pico-hydro

**Licence:** CC BY 4.0, Australian Survival LLM field notes. Orientation only. Not a fabrication manual and not a water licence.
**Confirm with:** your state water authority before you touch a creek, and the open manuals listed in AUSTRALIAN_SOURCES.md. Electrical work that connects to a switchboard is for a licensed electrician.

## What the creek can actually give you

Electrical power is roughly:

**watts ≈ flow (litres per second) × head (metres) × 9.8 × efficiency**

Head is the vertical drop from the intake water surface to the turbine, not the length of the pipe. Efficiency for a small, honest scheme is often around half. Anything that promises the full theoretical power is selling something.

Worked example. A creek you have measured at 10 litres a second, with 5 metres of fall, at 50 percent water-to-wire efficiency:

10 × 5 × 9.8 × 0.5 = about 245 watts, all day and all night, if the flow holds.

245 watts continuous is about 6 kilowatt-hours a day. That runs lights, a fridge, phone charging and a radio. It does not run a kettle, an oven and a welder at once. Measure the flow in the dry season, not the week after rain. Australian coastal creeks and tableland streams drop hard in summer or in a drought, depending on where you are. Design for the month you still want power.

Another example. 2 litres a second and 40 metres of fall, same efficiency:

2 × 40 × 9.8 × 0.5 = about 390 watts. High head and a small flow can beat a wide shallow creek. It needs a pipe that can hold the pressure.

## The parts, in words

An intake with a screen, so sticks and eels do not enter the pipe. A settling stretch if the water is sandy. A penstock, the pipe, which wants to be smooth, leak-free, and protected from stock and fire. A turbine matched to the site: a Pelton or similar for higher head, a crossflow or a propeller type for lower head. A generator. A load controller, also called a ballast or dump load, so that when you switch the lights off the voltage does not climb and cook what is left on. The dump load is usually a heater, often an air heater, bolted down and treated as permanently live equipment, not a spare bar radiator on a rug.

This note does not tell you how to wind a generator or weld a turbine. Those steps live in the manuals you obtain yourself. See AUSTRALIAN_SOURCES.md: Maher and Smith's pico-hydro manual, the NRCan buyer's guide, the NCAT/ATTRA beginners' guide, and Practical Action briefs. Their licences do not all allow this project to redistribute the PDFs. Put your copies in Your files.

## Australian constraints the overseas manuals skip

Water in a creek is not automatically yours to take because it crosses a paddock. State water law, harvestable-rights rules, and licences differ. A dam, a weir, or a pipe that takes a large share of a small stream can be illegal and can dry the creek for everything downstream, including fish. Several native fish need to move. A scheme that blocks a waterway without a passage is a problem even when the watts look good. Ask the state water agency and, where it applies, the fisheries agency, before you buy pipe.

Bushfire will melt poly pipe and destroy an unshaded controller. Flood will take a badly sited intake. Build for the creek you have in a wet week, not the creek in the photo.

## Electricity

Low-voltage DC and a small inverter is a different job from a long 230 volt run. Either way, mains-voltage wiring in a dwelling follows AS/NZS 3000 and is done by a licensed electrician. Do not parallel a hydro generator with a portable petrol generator and the grid on hope. One source at a time, through proper switchgear. The dump load and the inverter settings are what stop the voltage running away. If you do not understand that sentence for your own machine, you are not ready to switch it on. Put the controller manual in Your files and read it.

Rooftop solar and a creek can share a battery bank if someone who knows the gear designs the charge control. Two chargers fighting each other cook batteries. See the solar note.
