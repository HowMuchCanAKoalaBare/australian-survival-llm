# Radio, beacons and the ABC

**Licence:** CC BY 4.0, Australian Survival LLM field notes. Not an ACMA licence and not an AMSA registration.
**Confirm with:** the beacon registration on your own AMSA account, and the ABC frequency printed for the town you are actually in.

## UHF CB

On Australian UHF CB, channels **5** and **35** are for emergencies only. Channel 5 is the one you call on. Channel 35 is the repeater input. Using them for convoy chat is illegal and can block a real call, including a repeater you cannot hear. Nobody is required to be listening. A UHF radio is not a telephone call to 000. If you have mobile coverage, use the phone.

Channel 11 is the calling channel: make contact and move off it. Channel 40 is the highway channel. A handheld radio is line of sight, a few kilometres on flat ground and much less in a gully. A vehicle antenna does better. Hills, smoke and the ridge you are under all win.

On the older 27 MHz CB band, channel 9 is the emergency channel. Do not invent an HF frequency. If you have a registered outback HF radio, the frequencies are the ones that organisation gave you.

## ABC Local

The ABC is the national emergency broadcaster. In a fire, flood or cyclone, local ABC radio is where the warnings are read when the phone network is jammed or dark. The frequency is different in every district. Look it up before the bad day and write it on paper, in the glovebox and on the family plan. A battery radio or a wind-up radio works. The car radio works while the battery does. An app does not work when the mobile data does not.

## Beacons

A 406 MHz personal locator beacon is registered, free, with the Australian Maritime Safety Authority before the trip, against the person who carries it. An unregistered beacon still sends a distress, and then searchers waste time finding out who. Activate it when there is grave and imminent danger and you have no other way to get help. Leave it on, outside, with a clear view of the sky and the antenna up. Do not switch it off and on to "save the battery". Do not test it by setting it off.

A beacon with a return-link light can confirm that the alert was received. A basic one cannot. Either way, help takes time. An EPIRB is the marine version, registered to a boat, not to a hiker.

A satellite messenger (the brands that send texts) is a different thing. It needs a live subscription, and the SOS goes through a commercial centre. It is useful. A lapsed subscription is a brick. It does not replace telling someone your route and the time at which they should call for help if you have not checked in. See the remote-travel note.
